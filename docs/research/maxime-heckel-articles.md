# What Maxime Heckel's articles hold for Praxis

Researched 2026-11-21 for ticket #945 (parent #944, the world-systems track). The owner asked whether anything in the graphics articles on https://blog.maximeheckel.com is useful to Praxis now or later. This note reads the 18 graphics articles the blog listed that day, places each, and reads them a second time as explainers. It chooses no technique.

**Summary.** One question bears on M6, and it is the only *now* item. Two things are worth a line on the ladder's map at M7, and both rest on primary sources this note opened, not on the articles.

- **The GI article holds one thing for M6's charting to weigh against ADR 0020: probe visibility by a ray at shading time.** Its probe grid is a small version of DDGI, the technique ADR 0020 decides, with one difference that matters: where DDGI tests stored distance moments, the article traces from the shaded point to each probe and drops the hidden ones. By construction a traced test sees a wall of any thickness and would need no authored Interior volume; that is this note's inference, since the article's scenes have no thin wall. Its cost is 8 to 27 rays per pixel, which the article neither sources nor prices. It is a question to price or rule out at M6's charting, not a technique this note keeps. The article's other half, screen-space GI with a history buffer, is Temporal class (c), which ADR 0008 excludes and whose failures the author reports himself.
- **Sky, light shafts and clouds add no source** to `docs/research/volumetrics-without-screen-space-history.md`: the sky article follows Hillaire 2020, the clouds article names Schneider 2015 and Hillaire 2016, and the light-shaft article marches a shadow map, which ADR 0020 dropped.
- **Caustics, for M7's water.** The article points at Wallace's 2016 method: the sun's refracted wavefront drawn as a mesh, brightened by the ratio of its areas before and after. It is class (a), reads no shadow map and no screen history, and its author says it only works in certain cases and suits a planar body of water.
- **Refraction, for M7's transparency.** glTF's transmission extension recommends showing at least the opaque scene through a transmissive Material and mandates no technique. The screen-space form the article builds needs the opaque colour as a texture before the transmissive draw, and ADR 0019's lit and transparent passes share one render pass over a memoryless 4x colour attachment, so that copy does not exist. This is a constraint for M7's charting to price, read off the ADR, not a measured cost.
- **Licence.** The site's home page and feed state none, and it has no About page; its repository does: MIT for the source code, CC BY-NC 4.0 for the articles. Nothing was copied or adapted from either.
- **As explainers**, the articles show five practices a field guide page could take up inside its form: a stepped figure, a parameter widget on the mechanism's own constant, intermediate data shown as a picture with its axes read out, the failing image at the head of the section that fixes it, and a same-pixel before and after slider.

Of the 18 rows, 1 holds the *now* item (a clause proposed for the map's line on M6's charting), 2 become proposed lines for the ladder's map (#17), 5 are out of scope by the owner's ruling, and 10 are out because the engine or an earlier note already holds what they teach.

Sources: the 18 articles, each fetched on 2026-11-21 and read in full as prose, their code listings only where a row turns on one (the GI article's probe lookup); the papers, course notes and specifications they cite, where a row relies on one, each opened and quoted in `docs/references.toml`; the site's repository at the commit its footer linked. The entries of earlier notes cited here (DDGI 2019 and 2021, Hillaire 2020 and 2016, Schneider 2015, wgpu-hal's Metal device, and the dithering passage of Peters's page) were matched again against a fresh copy of their source on 2026-11-21. Nothing was measured, and no article's code was run.

Terms: the classes are `CONTEXT.md`'s Temporal classes.

## The GI article and M6

It holds one thing for M6's charting to weigh, and it is a question, not a sourced technique. When a pixel is shaded, the article traces from the surface toward each probe it blends and drops a probe that another object hides [ref:heckel-2026-gi-probe-occlusion-at-shading]. ADR 0020 answers the same problem, light leaking through walls, with what DDGI stores: distance moments per probe and a Chebyshev test at shading [ref:majercik-2019-ddgi-probe-encoding]. By ADR 0020 that test leaks where a wall is thinner than the probe spacing, hence its authored Interior volumes. By construction a traced test sees a wall of any thickness and would need no authoring; that is inference, since the article's scenes have no thin wall, and where every probe is hidden its lookup has nothing to blend. Its price is rays per pixel: 27 in the article, which blends a 3×3×3 neighbourhood, and 8 for the trilinear blend DDGI describes [ref:majercik-2019-ddgi-trilinear-blend] (2³, computed). The article cites no source for the test and gives no cost, and its scenes are spheres and boxes, with arches in the last. In Praxis it would be a compute pass, as the shadow mask is, since ADR 0019 moved the lit pass's rays out of its shader, and the nearest figure in hand is ADR 0020's for one sun ray per pixel, 1.8 to 2.0 ms on the M4 Pro's open scene, for a ray far longer than one that ends at a probe a spacing away. So it stands beside the GI survey's leak options (L1 to L3, none of which traces) as a fourth for M6's charting to price or rule out; its cost on the M4 Pro and the RTX 2070 SUPER is a prototype's question (ADR 0013).

The rest of the article is inside decisions already taken. Its screen-space half keeps a history texture, so it is class (c), and its author lists what ADR 0008 excludes it for: the history is reset on every camera movement or reprojected, which he does not like the look of, and nothing off camera is lit [ref:heckel-2026-gi-screen-space-drawbacks]. The article lists DDGI, the paper ADR 0020 is built on, among its sources; read against it, which is this note's reading, its grid is a simplification: a probe holds six estimates, one hemisphere per signed axis, where DDGI holds octahedral irradiance; its rays meet analytic spheres and boxes, not triangles; and it publishes no cost [ref:heckel-2026-gi-probe-form]. Three things it adds to a bare probe grid are each already on the map from a primary source: a history weight that is reset when the light changes, with ghosting left on abrupt changes, is the idea of DDGI 2021's change-triggered hysteresis, which also leaves ghosting, for small bright lights [ref:majercik-2021-ddgi-hysteresis-thresholds]; the weight starts at 0.95, inside the 0.85 to 0.98 DDGI 2019 gives [ref:majercik-2019-ddgi-hysteresis], and grows while the light is unchanged; sky radiance for a ray that misses, from a texture recomputed only when the sun moves, is the kind of cache the volumetrics note's option K1 names, a sky-space map that also feeds ray misses, though the article's holds a clear sky and K1's holds clouds; and a reflection ray whose hit is shaded from the probes [ref:heckel-2026-gi-sky-and-reflections] is within clause 10 of the GI survey's refresh (reflection cubemaps against radiance kept per probe). The author calls the result not production-grade and points at two other things. three.js's probe example is a grid of L2 spherical-harmonic probes baked by rendering a cube map at each probe [ref:threejs-light-probe-grid-baked]: row 2 of the GI survey, not ray-traced probes. Radiance cascades, which he has not yet looked into much, are ADR 0020's option E, whose reopen trigger is unchanged by a social post.

## What the other rows rest on

**2. Sky.** The article's last third implements Hillaire's tables and says where it stops short: aerial perspective is a 2D target per visible pixel where the paper has a volume, multiple scattering is skipped, and the sky-view table bands and flickers [ref:heckel-2026-sky-shortcuts]. The paper's volume is 32×32 over the screen with 32 depth slices over 32 km, which transparent surfaces in a forward pipeline read per vertex [ref:hillaire-2020-aerial-perspective-volume], and its multiple-scattering table is what it evaluates every frame without delay [ref:hillaire-2020-atmosphere-luts-every-frame]. Both are already technique 1 of the volumetrics note.

**3. Light shafts.** The march tests each step against a shadow map of the one light, moves its blue-noise offset every frame to hide the pattern, and flickers where the shadow map is coarse [ref:heckel-2025-light-shafts-shadow-map]. Praxis has no shadow map, and a pattern that changes each frame is the trap the volumetrics note names: without TAA nothing averages it. Hillaire's own advice for shafts in an atmosphere is a march with reprojection [ref:hillaire-2020-volumetric-shadows-reprojection]; what replaces that under ADR 0008 is the fog prototype's question.

**4. Clouds.** The article's two cost measures are a start offset per pixel from a blue-noise texture, animated over frames, and a half-resolution march upscaled with a bicubic filter, where its author sees little difference from full resolution [ref:heckel-2023-clouds-dither-and-upscale]. The filter's sources are an encyclopedia page and a Shadertoy, so nothing here can be relied on for the question K2 leaves open, what resolves a near march's jitter against 4x MSAA depth. The published forms are in the volumetrics note: Guerrilla's reprojected march, toward a target of about 2 ms on a PlayStation 4 [ref:schneider-2015-hzd-clouds], and Frostbite's remark that a low-resolution cloud layer meeting geometry needs a downsampled depth and a bilateral upsample [ref:hillaire-2016-frostbite-low-res-compositing-limit].

**5. Caustics.** The article draws a caustic for one glass-like mesh on a plane, calls it a cheat, credits the area-ratio step to Wallace, and reports that its plane sometimes clips the pattern [ref:heckel-2024-caustics-method]. Wallace's method is the part with reach. A mesh stands for the light's wavefront: each vertex is a ray traced along the sun's direction, refracted at the water surface and intersected with the ground, and a triangle's brightness is the ratio of its area before and after, which the fragment shader gets from screen-space derivatives of the two positions. Representing area directly, he writes, avoids the mass of samples that resolving a caustic's sharp creases otherwise takes. Its author says it "only works in certain cases" and suits "the refraction of light through a planar body of water"; he gives no cost [ref:wallace-2016-caustics-area-ratio]. The demo's source carries an MIT notice in its header, and the repository has no licence file [ref:wallace-2011-webgl-water-licence]. Fit, as inference from the ADRs: it is complete each frame; its output is one texture, in the light's space here, that the lit pass would read under water; and it would sit beside the shadow rays, as the cloud shadow map does in the volumetrics note, since one hard shadow ray toward the sun does not bend at the surface. Whether water is in the TLAS at all is the water survey's question.

**6. Refraction.** The article hides the mesh, draws the rest of the scene to a texture, and has the mesh sample that texture at an offset bent by its normal; dispersion is one index of refraction per channel, sampled in a loop its author calls a questionable choice for performance; drawing back faces first gives a second bend [ref:heckel-2023-refraction-scene-copy]. It cites no specification or paper for the refraction itself. The source Praxis would be held to is glTF. `KHR_materials_transmission` covers thin surfaces with no refraction, mandates no technique, and in a non-normative section recommends showing at least opaque objects through the transmissive surface [ref:khr-materials-transmission-opaque-behind]. Refraction proper is `KHR_materials_volume`'s: rays crossing a volume's boundary bend by the index of refraction [ref:khr-materials-volume-refraction]. `KHR_materials_dispersion` names tracing volume transmission once per colour channel with that channel's index as one real-time method [ref:khr-materials-dispersion-per-channel-ior]. The map's Materials line already defers transmission, volume and dispersion to the transparency line. What the article makes visible is the input: the screen-space form reads the opaque scene's colour as a texture. In ADR 0019 the lit and transparent passes share one render pass, whose 4x colour is transient and memoryless on Apple GPUs [ref:wgpu-30-metal-memoryless] and is resolved at the end of it, so no opaque-only colour exists to read. A second pass after a resolve, or a refraction ray traced by analogy with the map's mirror reflection (one ray, class a), are the two shapes this note can see; neither is priced anywhere in the sources read.

**13. Dithering.** The article treats dither as a look: a pixel becomes black or white by comparing its luminance with a threshold from white noise, a Bayer matrix or a blue-noise texture [ref:heckel-2024-dithering-as-style]. Praxis's tonemapping pass dithers to hide 8-bit banding, with a blue-noise mask remapped to triangular noise as Peters describes [ref:peters-blue-noise] (`src/render/shaders/display.wgsl`). The article cites no paper and adds nothing to that.

**Two techniques inside the out-of-scope articles** serve a photoreal renderer, and neither adds anything. *Moebius-style post-processing* finds edges with a Sobel filter over the depth and normal buffers; Praxis finds its edges per sample, in 4x MSAA and in the shadow mask's edge rays, and a depth-aware upsample for a low-resolution cloud march is already named in the volumetrics note. *Shading Motion* estimates motion by differencing frames, which its author calls guesswork, then for motion blur adds a velocity map built from each object's projected centre, since a renderer knows its motion; he calls that part a hack. Motion blur is on the map until content needs it, and Praxis already carries each moving entity's previous transform (`PreviousWorldTransform`).

## As explainers

A field guide page already opens each section with its key idea, quotes the code at a commit, and shows before and after crops (`docs/agents/workflow.md`, PRs). No page under `docs/guides/` has a live demo today. Five practices from the articles fit that form:

1. **A stepped figure.** One diagram advanced a state at a time, with a caption per state: the probe diagram in [Lighting That Feels Just Right](https://blog.maximeheckel.com/posts/lighting-that-feels-just-right/) has eleven ("Step 1 of 11"). A page that follows data through phases (the culling pass's two phases, the shadow mask's second surface) could show the same figure once per phase, as static frames.
2. **A widget on the mechanism's own constant.** The same article's hysteresis slider shows the one trade the constant makes, noise at one end and lag at the other. This is the case the page form allows a live demo for, where it shows the mechanism itself: M6's probe hysteresis is the first candidate.
3. **Intermediate data as a picture with its axes read out.** [On Rendering the Sky](https://blog.maximeheckel.com/posts/on-rendering-the-sky-sunsets-and-planets/) prints the transmittance table and says what each axis and each shade means; [On Shaping Light](https://blog.maximeheckel.com/posts/shaping-light-volumetric-lighting-with-post-processing-and-raymarching/) prints the shadow map at two resolutions. A page whose mechanism writes a target (the depth pyramid, the shadow mask's bits, a Froxel's light count) could print that target the same way.
4. **The failing image at the head of the section that fixes it.** Each section starts from the artefact the last one left: 8 rays of noise before accumulation, layering before the blue-noise offset in [Real-time dreamy Cloudscapes](https://blog.maximeheckel.com/posts/real-time-cloudscapes-with-volumetric-raymarching/). A page's before crop could sit beside the section's key idea, not only in *Why this way*.
5. **A same-pixel before and after slider.** The GI article compares one frame with and without the effect under a draggable divider. The guides' crops sit side by side; a divider over two aligned images lets the eye hold one pixel. It is a comparison control, not a live demo.
