Praxis · M3 · ticket #189 · PR #219

From sunlight to an 8-bit pixel

In brief

Real light spans about 30 stops (doublings), from a moonless night to direct sun, but an 8-bit display has 256 levels per channel. This guide follows the survey behind Praxis's answer (PR #219): lights in real units, a hand-set exposure per scene, a 16-bit float target, a weighted MSAA resolve that keeps bright edges thin, Gran Turismo 7's tone curve, and blue-noise dither after the sRGB encode. The idea to keep: expose light before storing it, map it onto the display once at the end, and dither in 8-bit steps.

You'll learn

  • What lux, candela, nits and EV100 measure
  • Why fp16 needs pre-exposure and a clamp at 65504
  • How tone curves differ on the path to white
  • How reversible weighting fixes bright edges under 4x MSAA
  • Why dither goes after the sRGB encode

Read first: nothing; it stands on its own.

Chapter 1

The problem

When the survey was written, the forward pass wrote linear radiance straight into an 8-bit sRGB target. There was no exposure and no tonemapping operator, so anything brighter than 1.0 clipped, channel by channel. The lights were relative: a sun of about 1, unitless point lights, a constant ambient term.

That worked while scenes stayed dim. The five scenes the probe captured peaked at 855 (helmet) and 499.9 (pbr-sweep), yet no more than 0.56% of any one's pixels passed 1.0. Real light spans about 30 stops, each stop a doubling, from a moonless night to direct sun. A display shows a small slice of that. Getting from one to the other takes a chain of steps, and the survey has one decision for each link.

Chapter 2 · decision U

Light in numbers

Physics measures visible light with four photometric units. glTF, the format Praxis imports, already uses three of them:

  • Illuminance, in lux (lx): light arriving on a surface, lumens per square metre. The sun is a directional light in lux.
  • Luminous intensity, in candela (cd): light leaving a point in one direction, lumens per steradian. glTF point and spot lights use candela.
  • Luminance, in cd/m², also called nits: how bright a surface looks. Emissive surfaces, the sky and displays are measured in it.
  • Luminous flux, in lumens (lm): a lamp's total output. Frostbite, Filament and Bevy author point lights in lumens, as Godot does when its physical light units are turned on, so Bevy's glTF loader multiplies candela by 4π.

Option U1 puts Praxis's lights in these units, exactly as glTF defines them: directional lights in lux, point lights in candela, emissive and background in cd/m², ambient in lux, exposure as EV100. glTF lights then import unchanged, and the ratios between sun, sky and lamps are real, which is what ray-traced shadows and probe GI (ADR 0020) will expect. Option U2 keeps relative numbers: fewer stops to handle and an exposure near 1, but glTF lights need a scale at import. Frostbite moved to physical units mid-life and called converting its relative lighting rigs "extremely hard". Units are hard to change once worlds are authored, which is why U1 is recorded in an ADR, ADR 0023.

Chapter 3 · decision E

Exposure: choosing which slice to show

A camera does not record light as it is. It picks a slice: aperture, shutter time and sensor sensitivity set how much light becomes a mid-grey photo. Engines copy this with one number, EV100, the exposure value at ISO 100:

EV100 = log2(N² / t) − log2(S / 100) for f-number N, shutter time t in seconds and ISO S. A pixel's exposed value is its luminance times 1 / (1.2 · 2^EV100), so 1.0 lands near display white. The 1.2 comes from how camera sensors are rated. The photographer's Sunny 16 rule lands near EV100 15.

One EV cannot fit everything. Move the slider and watch two 18% grey cards, one in a living room and one in direct sun.

Three ways to choose the EV:

  • E1, manual: each Reference scene sets its EV100. It is exact every frame, keeps Goldens exact and costs one multiply.
  • E2, histogram adaptation: a compute pass measures the frame's brightness and eases the exposure toward it, as eyes adapt. Its state is one number carried across frames, which ADR 0008 allows, since nothing smears on screen. The probe measured 0.27 ms at 1440p. The catch is lag and flicker: Valve found blinking lights made Source's exposure oscillate.
  • E3, local tone mapping: different exposure per region of the image, for scenes that join a dark interior and sunlight, at the risk of halos. Nothing in M3 needs it.
Chapter 4 · decision R, part one

Storing it: floating-point targets

Exposed values can be 0.001 or 3,000, so the lit pass needs a target that stores floats. A 16-bit float (fp16, Rgba16Float) has 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits. Its largest value is 65504, its smallest normal value 6.1×10⁻⁵, and from there up its steps are at most 0.1% of the value. Rg11b10Ufloat packs three unsigned floats into 32 bits, half the memory, with 6-, 6- and 5-bit mantissas and no alpha. The coarse blue is where Frostbite saw a small hue shift.

Bright values still overflow. A glossy non-metal's highlight of the sun at 100,000 lx, at roughness 0.04 and with the sun as a directional light, peaks at 1.24×10⁸ cd/m², 10.9 stops past fp16's maximum. Engines therefore pre-expose: shading runs in f32 and only the exposed result is stored. At EV100 15 that highlight becomes 3,162. A white metal's highlight becomes 79,053, still too big, so every option clamps to 65504 before the write. That clamp matters across backends: the probe measured that the M4 Pro saturates overflow to 65504, a case Metal's specification leaves open, while Vulkan's rules allow infinity, and an infinite sample can turn into black patches.

Chapter 5 · decision O

Tonemapping: squeezing light onto a screen

A tonemapping operator is a curve from exposed values, 0 to infinity, onto the display's 0 to 1. A good one keeps mid-tones close to what the scene had, rolls highlights off smoothly instead of clipping them, and decides how bright colours behave near white.

That last point separates the families. Apply the curve to each channel and a bright saturated colour shifts hue: the brightest channel tops out while the others keep climbing, so a bright blue turns cyan or magenta, or stays a saturated block that never whitens. Apply it to luminance only and the hue holds, but saturated highlights leave the display's range. Film and cameras do something in between: very bright coloured light desaturates toward white. Engines call that the path to white.

Pick an operator below. The sweep is a small copy of the probe's: fully saturated hues across, a neutral strip at the left, and exposure from 0.18 × 2¹⁰ at the top down to 0.18 × 2⁻⁶ at the bottom.

The probe ran all nine operators over the full sweep and over the scenes it captured, as they were then, at the same exposure. Brightness differences between tiles come from where each operator puts mid-grey; look at how colours behave as they get bright.

PR #219's short list for O, all analytic, permissively licensed and taking saturated light to white (the survey note itself lists five options, Tony McMapface and an ACES 1 fit among them, and recommends none):

  • GT7 (recommended): Gran Turismo 7's operator, published at SIGGRAPH 2025 with MIT code. Nearly linear mid-tones, hue held there, saturated light to white near the peak. Of these three, it is the only one built for both SDR and any HDR peak, so it would carry over if Praxis ever outputs HDR. The most expensive, at 0.29 ms; it can be baked into a lookup table when grading arrives.
  • AgX: Blender 4.0's default. A softer, desaturated filmic look; the three.js form the probe ran is SDR only, and Blender 5.0 added an HDR view with a 1000-nit peak.
  • PBR Neutral: Khronos's operator for product rendering. Base colours come through most faithfully, with highlights squeezed into the top codes. SDR only.

Not recommended: the ACES 1 fits (Hill's whitens blue through magenta, and Narkowicz's over-saturates brights), Tony McMapface (a lookup table with no HDR variant), and Reinhard and Hable (saturated colours never reach white).

Chapter 6 · decision R, part two

Bright edges and the 4x MSAA resolve

With 4x MSAA every pixel has four samples, and the hardware averages them into one value: the resolve. ADR 0019 keeps those samples in fast on-chip memory and never writes them out (the attachment is memoryless), so the hardware average is the only resolve on offer.

Averaging is right for light but wrong for what you see. Say a thin wire of brightness 100 covers one of a pixel's four samples and the background is 0.05. The average is 25: still far above white, so the edge pixel turns fully white, as if the wire covered all of it. Bright sub-pixel detail becomes thick white stair steps.

The fix (Karis, 2013) is reversible weighting. Each shader in the lit pass writes c / (1 + luminance), which keeps every sample's luminance under 1, though a saturated colour's channels can stay above 1: pure blue of 100 is stored as 12.2. The hardware averages those, and the final pass undoes the squeeze with c / (1 − luminance). The result behaves like averaging after tonemapping: the same pixel resolves to 0.40.

The options for R:

  • R2 (recommended): Rgba16Float with the weighting. It measured 0.009 ms extra in the lit pass and 0.018 ms in the final pass, and keeps ADR 0019's memoryless 4x. Two costs: sub-pixel highlights lose energy, and M6's transparent passes would blend in the weighted space, which a fog line records. fp16 is required, since the inverse of the largest value under 1 caps at 2047 in fp16 but at 127 in 11-bit floats.
  • R1: Rgba16Float with the plain box resolve. Physically averaged, stair-stepped at bright edges.
  • R3: store the samples and resolve them in a shader. 117,964,800 bytes at 1440p, and it reopens ADR 0019.
Chapter 7 · decision D

Banding and dither

An 8-bit surface has 256 levels per channel. A smooth, dim gradient covers only a few of them, so it shows as bands. Dither adds a little noise before rounding, so neighbouring pixels round up or down in the right proportion and the average comes out right. The eye sees fine grain instead of bands.

The details matter. Triangular noise spanning 2 steps peak to peak is the least noise that keeps the error's strength the same whatever the signal, so the grain does not itself vary with brightness (Lipshitz et al.). It must be added after the sRGB encode, in 8-bit steps: added in linear light it is 12.92 times too strong near black. And the pattern must stay fixed on screen. ADR 0008 rules out averaging frames, and a fixed pattern keeps Goldens exact.

Blue noise is noise without low frequencies: its dots are spread evenly, so it reads as finer grain than white noise. Ordered (Bayer) patterns are cheap but show a regular grid. The recommended mask is Christoph Peters's free 64 × 64 blue-noise texture (CC0), the same one used below.

The options for D: D1 (recommended), static triangular dither from Peters's mask, added in the encoded domain through a non-sRGB view of the surface; the mask joins assets/manifest.json. An integer hash gives white noise without adding an asset. D2: none, and dark gradients band. The probe measured the dither at a few hundredths of a millisecond on top of the operator.

Chapter 8 · decision H

Colour spaces and displays

A colour space is three things: which red, green and blue its primaries are, which white they balance to, and a transfer function between stored numbers and light. sRGB uses the Rec. 709 primaries, the D65 white, and a curve that spends more codes on dark tones, where eyes are sensitive.

Shading and blending must happen in linear light, where numbers add like light does. Encoding happens once, at the end. The …Srgb texture formats do the encode in hardware on write, which is why the dither step must write to a format that does not: it encodes itself, adds noise in 8-bit steps, then writes the codes as they are. The survey proposed a non-sRGB view of the sRGB surface; since #255 the final pass writes a Bgra8Unorm or Rgba8Unorm surface instead, because that view needs a wgpu downlevel flag outside the Capability floor.

HDR displays

SDR output is relative: 1.0 is the display's white, whatever its brightness. HDR output adds room above that white, and each platform names it differently:

  • EDR (Apple): linear values where 1.0 is SDR white and brighter values go up to the display's current headroom. A Pro Display XDR at 1,600 nits peak and 500 nits SDR white has 3.2. Above it, values clip.
  • scRGB (Windows): linear fp16 with sRGB primaries, where 1.0 means 80 nits.
  • PQ (BT.2100, HDR10): an absolute curve up to 10,000 nits with Rec. 2020 primaries. BT.2100 maps fp16 1.0 to 203 cd/m², and televisions clip long before 10,000. HLG is its relative broadcast sibling.

wgpu 30.0.1 can already configure all of these, through SurfaceConfiguration::color_space, and reports the display's headroom. It writes no HDR10 metadata. The M4 Pro's built-in display is an XDR panel, so HDR output could be tried on the reference machine. But an HDR path needs an operator that takes a display peak, as GT7 does, and its own testing.

The options for H: H1 (recommended), SDR only in M3, with HDR output a fog line. H2: EDR on Metal now, a second output path, with Goldens staying SDR.

Chapter 9 · decision B

Bloom

Bright light seems to glow, because lenses and eyes scatter a little of it into its surroundings. A display cannot show a light brighter than white, so bloom adds that glow to say "brighter than white". The usual form (Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare, 2014) blurs the image down a chain of ever smaller copies and adds the result back. It uses no brightness threshold: the eye's scattering has none, and a threshold breaks energy conservation. The probe measured that chain at 0.27 ms at 1440p.

The options for B: B1 (recommended), none in M3, with the measured cost kept in a fog line. #200's size ceiling, about 400 changed lines, would make bloom a ticket of its own anyway. B2, the Call of Duty chain, and B3, physically based glare by FFT convolution, are later polish.

The seven decisions

PR #219 put these seven questions to the project's owner, who took every recommendation on 2026-10-20. The answers are recorded on the project's map, U1 became the light-units ADR, ADR 0023, before the PR merged, and #200 carried out the rest, with one change to D1: the final pass writes an 8-bit UNORM surface in place of a non-sRGB view (chapter 8). Each card gives PR #219's recommendation first, which is also what was chosen.

Glossary

Stop
A doubling or halving of light; log2 of a ratio.
Lux, candela, nit
Light on a surface, light from a point in one direction, and how bright a surface looks (cd/m²).
EV100
Exposure value at ISO 100; one step is one stop.
Exposed value
Luminance times the exposure; 1.0 is about display white.
Pre-exposure
Applying the exposure before storing, so values fit in fp16.
fp16
A 16-bit float: 10-bit mantissa, largest value 65504.
Tonemapping operator
A curve from exposed values onto the display's 0 to 1.
Path to white
How bright saturated colours desaturate toward white.
Per-channel, hue-preserving
A curve applied to R, G and B separately, or to brightness alone.
Resolve
Averaging a pixel's MSAA samples into one value.
Memoryless
An attachment kept only in on-chip tile memory, never written out.
Reversible weighting
Writing c/(1 + luminance) before the resolve and undoing it after.
Banding
Visible steps where a smooth gradient has too few output levels.
Dither, blue noise
Noise added before rounding; blue noise has no low frequencies.
Colour space
Primaries, a white point and a transfer function.
Gamut
The range of colours a set of primaries can show.
Linear, encoded
Numbers proportional to light, or squeezed by a transfer curve for storage.
Headroom
On an HDR display, peak brightness over SDR white.
Paper white
The brightness given to SDR white inside an HDR signal.
Bloom
A glow added around very bright light, to signal brighter than white.
