A town built to be measured

In brief

Milestone M3 must judge Praxis on a scene as dense as an open world, before the engine can stream an authored one. Ticket #206 generates town: 254,256 Instances, 10,000 of them circling birds, with every random choice hashed from its place and what it decides, so every launch builds the same town. Its median frame exceeds both the GPU and the CPU Budget. The idea to keep: hash each value from its own parts, not one shared stream, so changing one choice moves only what it decides.

You'll learn

  • How per-choice hashes place a world deterministically
  • How a camera path decides what a Baseline measures
  • Why a shader with side effects defeats early depth tests
  • What 4x MSAA does, and does not do, for sub-pixel wires
  • How a build overlapping the frame before was counted twice

Chapter 1 · ADRs 0013 and 0019

Why a dense scene

Until town, each Reference scene tested one feature on a few Instances. M3 has to answer two questions at the scale Praxis is built for: do ADR 0013's Budgets hold, and does ADR 0019's main pass stay under its gate? Both need a scene that loads the culling pass, the lit pass and the TLAS the way an open world will. Town is that scene, and code generates it, because the path that will fill real worlds with authored content, M4's Placements, does not exist yet.

The two questions are the first two of M3's exit criteria, and the third follows from them:

  • Dense scene. In the median frame of its camera path, at 2560 × 1440 with 4x MSAA, town must draw at least 100,000 Instances and 5,000,000 triangles after culling, and stay within ceilings of 12 ms on its GPU frame, 6 ms on its main-thread critical path and 8 GB of GPU memory. ADR 0013 made its Budgets provisional until the first Baseline of a Reference scene at target scale, and named M3's dense scene as that scene: the Budgets are re-decided against town's first Baseline.
  • ADR 0019's gate. The clustered forward main pass stays while its GPU time, from the first culling dispatch to the end of the lit pass with shadow rays off, has a median under 6 ms over the scene's camera path at 1440p with 4x MSAA. A breach blocks M3's exit until two other designs, ADR 0019's options B and D, are prototyped on town and the project's owner decides.
  • GPU-driven draws. Draw calls per pipeline and Hi-Z phase stay constant whatever the Instance count, and with culling off town must match culling on within Golden tolerance at frames spread along its path. That is why the frame's draw must hold the whole town (chapter 5).

Why generated

The engine's real content will arrive as Placements, Mesh Assets positioned inside Chunks of a streamed Game world, and M4 builds that path. M3's measurements cannot wait for it, and a generated scene needs nothing authored. Town's 19 Meshes are built by code at cook time (generators::meshes()), cooked into Blobs in the Cooked cache under Asset IDs derived from the scene's name, and loaded like any other Mesh. Its Instances are placed when the scene spawns, by fixed rules and by values hashed from where each stands (chapter 2).

Generating the scene also keeps it fixed. A Reference scene pairs a Scene with a fixed camera path and injected time so that every run produces the same frames, and town's hash gives its content the same guarantee: every launch places the same 244,256 static Instances in the same places, so its Goldens and Baselines always compare the same town.

Chapter 2 · mix and random

Placing 254,256 Instances the same way every time

Town is a grid of blocks, and every choice in it, from whether a block is a park to what stands on each cell of a roof, is a random value: a number from 0 up to 1, hashed from the block's column and row, what the value decides and which item it decides for. The same four parts always give the same number, whatever else is decided and in whatever order, so every launch places the same town.

The town is 1 × 1 km: 20 × 20 blocks on a 50 m pitch, with 12 m streets between them and along the edge. A block is a park with a 12% chance, and a park holds 5 × 5 trees and 4 benches. Every other block holds 4 buildings, each 16 m square and of 3 to 6 storeys of 3.2 m, with windows on its two street fronts and plant on its roof. Each block's 2.5 m sidewalks carry 4 street trees a side, and on some sides bollards, a bench or a bin; each side has 6 parking places, each holding a car with a 70% chance.

mix comes from SplitMix64. As a generator, SplitMix64 adds a fixed odd constant, the paper's golden gamma, to its state at each step, and returns the state scrambled by two rounds of xor-shift and multiply and a final xor-shift; Vigna's splitmix64.c gives the constants. mix(seed) is one such step from the state seed, used as a hash, so nearby seeds give values that look unrelated. random folds its four parts through mix one at a time, each combined with the hash so far, and keeps the result's high bits, as many as an f64 holds exactly, as a fraction of 1.

The four parts say where and what: the block's column and row, the Choice, and an item number for which building, roof cell, side or car the value is about. A roof cell's item is its building's index shifted above the cell's (building << 8 | cell), so each of a block's roof cells has an item of its own. The shares then turn a value into a decision: a roof cell whose value is below 0.30 holds an air conditioner, one from 0.30 up to 0.52 a vent, and so on up to the 5% of cells that stay empty.

The lamps use the same idea across the whole town. Each of the 1,600 block corners gets a random rank, the corners are sorted by rank, and the 1,024 lowest get a lamp. Which corners win depends only on their ranks, so the order in which the blocks were visited does not matter.

Why no value may depend on another

The usual alternative is one stream of random numbers, consumed in placement order. Then each value depends on how many were drawn before it. One more bench in the first block, a loop that visits a block's sides in another order, or a park share moved from 12% would shift the stream under the values after it, and the town beyond the change would come out different, at least until the stream happened to fall back into step: new Goldens and new Baselines for a change that touched one thing, with no way to tell its own effect from the shuffle. A value hashed from its own parts depends on nothing else. A change to one choice moves only what that choice decides, and the town could be placed in parallel, or Chunk by Chunk, and come out the same.

One order does matter. A value hashes its choice by its position in Choice, so a new choice joins the end of the enum, as part 3's Flock and Bird did. Inserted earlier, it renumbers every choice after it, and every value of those choices changes.

Part 2 did exactly that, on purpose, to fix a shared seed. One Yaw choice used to turn a roof's plant, the street trees and the park trees, and the plant's items (building << 8 | cell) and the street trees' (side << 8 | tree) overlap: the plant on cells 0 to 3 of building n's roof turned exactly as side n's four street trees did. Splitting Yaw into PlantYaw, StreetTreeYaw and ParkTreeYaw gave each its own seeds, and inserting the two new choices before Car renumbered Car through Lamp. The roofs, which blocks are parks and the storeys stayed as they were; the street and park trees turned differently, the cars, bollards, benches and bins came out different and the lamps moved, and the static town went from 243,907 Instances to 244,256.

Chapter 3 · 19 Meshes

What the town is made of

Town draws 19 Meshes, all built by code at cook time: a ground plane, and the rest from three shapes, boxes, cylinders that narrow into cones, and a tube for the wires. One of them, a unit box drawn with a Material per Instance, serves every slab, wall, window and rail, so one BLAS holds them all. The lamps hang their lights outside their fixtures, the wires are thinner than a pixel from a few tens of metres away, and 10,000 birds circle over the roofs in flocks: the town's Moving Instances.

Three shapes

Apart from the ground, a plane the scenes module shares (ground_plane), the generators in town.rs build every Mesh from a few parts, merged into one Primitive per Material. cuboid makes a box with its own vertices on each face, so its edges stay sharp. cylinder makes a cone's frustum from a bottom radius to a top radius, smooth round its side and capped at each end that has a radius; a top radius of 0 makes a cone, as in tree crowns, a vent's cap and a water tank's roof. wire makes the wires.

One box, many Materials

Slabs, buildings, windows and rails are all one Mesh, TownMesh::Box, "a unit cube on its base, scaled per Instance and drawn with its Instance's Material". The spawn gives each box a MaterialRef, which overrides the Mesh's own Materials, as a Renderable may: concrete or grass for the slabs, one of four plasters for the walls, glass for the windows and steel for the rails. Every other Mesh's Primitives name their own Materials. A BLAS is built per Mesh, so all the boxes share one, and the TLAS places it once per Instance, with that Instance's scale.

Windows

Each building has windows on its two street fronts, 4 columns per storey, each a box of 1.4 × 1.8 × 0.12 m centred on its storey. Half of each box's depth stands proud of the wall, so its glass is a surface of its own in front of the plaster rather than one in the same plane.

Roofs

Each roof has a railing of 20 posts a side and a rail along each side, and inside it a 6 × 6 grid of 2.2 m cells. Each cell's plant comes from a random value and the shares of chapter 2's demo: an air conditioner 30% of the time, a vent 22%, a solar panel 24%, a water tank 6%, a dish 7% and an aerial 6%, leaving 5% of cells empty. Each plant also takes a random turn about the vertical.

Streets

Along each side of a block, the sidewalk carries 4 street trees; 30% of sides have a row of bollards every 2 m, 30% a bench and 40% a bin. In the street, 6 parking places a side each hold a car with a 70% chance, red or blue. Utility poles stand every 25 m on one side of every street, in both directions, and each span between two poles holds 2 wires, 7.6 and 7.0 m up.

Lamps: the light outside the fixture

The 1,024 lamps stand at block corners, each with its arm over the street, and each holds one point light, hung 0.15 m below the lamp's head and so outside the lamp's Mesh. #205, which gave point lights their shadow rays, asked for that placement. Each point light traces its own shadow ray from the lit surface to the light, and a light inside its fixture's Mesh would meet the fixture on every ray: hidden from everything it lights.

Each light is warm white, (1.0, 0.85, 0.65), with a range of 18 m. Its intensity is 20 times the sun's illuminance, read in candela: a point light of I candela gives I / d2 lux at d metres, so at 4.5 m it gives about the sun's 100,000 lux, and nearly as much on the ground below, so its pool of light shows in daylight.

Wires: thinner than a pixel

A wire hangs from one pole to the next as a tube of 3 faces, 1.5 cm in radius, over 15 straight segments, sagging 0.6 m at mid-span along a parabola. A hanging cable's exact shape is a catenary; for a sag this small against its span, the parabola is the catenary's shallow-sag form. From a few tens of metres away, the wire is thinner than a pixel, which makes it town's test of 4x MSAA (chapter 7).

Flocks: the Moving Instances

Part 3 adds town's Moving Instances: 100 flocks on a 10 × 10 grid 100 m apart, each centre up to 20 m from its cell's middle along each axis, which keeps the whole flock inside its cell, and 30 to 55 m up, clear of every roof's plant, turning at 0.2 to 0.6 rad a second one way or the other. Each flock is 100 birds, 24-triangle pigeons of two boxes, a body and a pair of wings, placed 6 to 30 m from its centre, evenly over the ring's area, and up to 4 m above or below it, each facing the way it circles. All of it comes from the same hash, with the flock's column and row on its own grid and the choices Flock and Bird.

No new system moves them. A flock's centre is an entity with the existing Spin component, which the fixed schedule turns about +Y, setting each rotation from the Tick count so that no rounding accumulates. Each bird is the centre's child (ChildOf), so it circles as the centre turns. The centre and every bird carry PreviousWorldTransform, which ADR 0022 asks of every entity whose World transform the fixed schedule changes, children included. So frames draw them blended between their two newest Ticks, and each bird is a Moving Instance. There are exactly 10,000, the capacity of the dynamic TLAS, and a compile-time assertion keeps the flocks' count equal to it, so town always measures the dynamic TLAS full.

Why rigid flocks

A flock is rigid: its birds keep their places about the centre, and the whole ring turns. That keeps the simulation to almost nothing. Each Tick, spin sets 100 rotations, and the fixed schedule's last system, propagate_world_transforms, composes each bird's World transform from its parent's, as it does for any entity whose ancestor moved. The renderer cannot tell the difference. Prepare rewrites all 10,000 bird Instances every frame, 128 bytes each, and the dynamic TLAS is rebuilt every frame from 10,000 entries, each placed by its own transform, just as if every bird flew a path of its own. What town measures is the renderer's cost of 10,000 Moving Instances, not a flocking simulation's, and it measures that at the load M3's sixth exit criterion judges the dynamic TLAS at.

Seen from the street, the birds barely register: they change 35 of the Golden's 230,400 pixels, by up to 81 8-bit steps in a channel, on Metal and on software Vulkan alike. Their cost is in the dynamic TLAS and the rays traced against it, not in drawing. In full runs on the M4 Pro they add about 5 ms to the GPU frame, most of it the dynamic TLAS's build as the frame times it, and about 10,000 heap allocations a frame; #207 later found that the build ran almost wholly alongside the previous frame's lit and tonemapping passes, so the frame counted that time twice. Chapter 8 has the figures.

What checks it

  • tests/goldens.rs: town joins the scenes captured there, so its Golden is checked, culling off against on, Froxel lights against brute force with shadow rays off, and the 100 km shift by whole 64 m cells, on Metal and on software Vulkan. When part 3 merged, the two comparisons ran at frame 59; since #298, merged on 2026-10-26 as fd2ee9b, they run at eight frames along town's path, from 59 to 1,800, and on software Vulkan the culling comparison allows one differing pixel a frame.
  • tests/frame_stats.rs: the closed-form counts above with culling off, so the frame's draw holds the whole town and nothing overflows; and after the first frame, the birds' 10,000 Instances written every frame, none of them left out of the dynamic TLAS, and the frame's uploads to the byte.
  • src/cook/mesh.rs's tests: every Reference scene's Mesh, town's included, cooks into valid Meshlets.
Chapter 4 · camera_at

The camera path

A Reference scene's figures are medians over its camera path, so the path decides what they measure. Town's path starts at eye level in a street, rises over the rooftops and circles over the town, 480 m from its middle and 260 m up, and its position and speed never jump. The bench measures frames 248 to 2047, and from frame 780 on the camera is on the circle, so most measured frames, and the median frame that the Budgets and the gate are judged on, look out over the town rather than down one street.

No cuts

The rise is a cubic Hermite curve: the cubic fixed by a position and a velocity at each end. With t its progress from 0 to 1, p0 the street's end, p1 the circle's start, and m0 the street's velocity times the rise's length in frames:

position(t) = p0 (2t³ − 3t² + 1) + m0 (t³ − 2t² + t) + p1 (3t² − 2t³) = p0 (1 − arrived) + m0 (t³ − 2t² + t) + p1 arrived, arrived = 3t² − 2t³ position'(0) = m0 position'(1) = 0

The fourth term of the Hermite form, the velocity at the far end, is zero, so the rise leaves at the street's speed and arrives at rest. The code names the weight of the circle's start arrived, since it grows from 0 to 1 as the camera arrives, and the gaze uses it too, to slide its target from far up the avenue to the town's middle. On the circle, the angle grows with the square of the frames for the first 120, so the speed rises evenly from rest to 0.0006 rad a frame, and then stays there. Position and speed are continuous from the first frame to the last, as the demo's speed plot shows.

Why the median frame is an orbit frame

The bench renders the frames before 248 to let allocations and the GPU's clocks settle, then measures frames 248 to 2047 at 2560 × 1440. The street ends at frame 419 and the rise at 779, so every measured frame from 780 to 2047 is on the circle, and those are most of the run. The median frame is therefore one round the town, and that is the frame M3's first exit criterion and ADR 0019's gate are judged on: the Baseline's median draws 137,730 of the town's 254,256 Instances, more than half. The Golden's frame, 59, is a street frame the bench's timings never include: town's Golden checks the image, and its Baseline the median. Every run also records the counters of frames 8 to 71, street frames too, so that runs of any length compare on them, and CI benches town on those alone (--ci-counters, at 640 × 360), since town is too dense for CI's software Vulkan to render its usual run in time.

Chapter 5 · DrawCapacity

Culling at target scale

Each frame, the culling pass builds the frame's draw on the GPU from the Instances whose bounds reach into the view and that the Hi-Z pyramid does not find hidden. In the street it draws 5,608 of the town's 254,256 Instances; in the bench's median frame, round the town, it draws 137,730, clear of M3's floor of 100,000. The draw must also hold the whole town with culling off, which the Goldens compare with culling on, so part 1 doubled its capacity to 225 packed indices: 128 MiB, the most one storage binding holds at the Capability floor.

Why the whole town must fit

The draw is a list of packed indices, each a u32 holding a Meshlet record in its high bits and a vertex within that Meshlet in its low bits (ADR 0019), three to a triangle. The old capacity, 224 indices, holds 5.59 million triangles, and the whole town is 9.99 million with the flocks, so with culling off the old draw would overflow. The culling pass then ends the draw at the last Instance that fits, and Frame stats count the rest as Instances the draw had no room for. The image with culling off would lose them all and differ from the image with culling on. 225 indices hold 11.18 million triangles, room for the whole town.

The ceiling: one storage binding

The culling pass writes the indices through one storage binding, and ADR 0021 keeps max_storage_buffer_binding_size at wgpu's default of 128 MiB, which is Vulkan's minimum and lavapipe's maxStorageBufferRange. lavapipe is CI's software Vulkan, which renders every Golden on that backend, so a floor above its limit would refuse it. 225 indices of 4 bytes fill the 128 MiB exactly: the draw cannot grow again without splitting it across bindings or raising the floor. Its size stays provisional until M4 splits the GPU memory Budget.

Chapter 6 · the light count pass

Lights per lit pixel, and the side-effect trap

Town's 1,024 point lights are culled into Froxels, and the lit pass shades each pixel with the lights its Froxel lists. To see what that costs, part 1 counts, at every lit pixel, the lights listed there and those whose range actually reaches the surface. The first attempt counted inside the lit pass and counted up to 4.7 times the pixels there were, because a shader with side effects runs for fragments that the depth test then throws away. The count now runs in a compute pass after the frame, from the depth the lit pass keeps.

Counting in the lit pass

The lit pass already walks each pixel's Froxel list, so the first attempt added atomics to its fragment shader. With 4x MSAA, a pixel on a triangle's edge runs the shader once for each triangle that covers part of it, so the count was kept to the fragment that covers the pixel's first sample, sample 0. On a 48 × 48 test of one surface, that counted exactly. On town, at 640 × 360 and counting a lattice of 1 pixel in 16, it counted 2.8 times as many pixels as the lattice holds with culling on, and 4.7 times with culling off.

Why: side effects move the depth test late

A GPU would rather test depth before it runs a fragment shader and skip the shader for fragments that fail, and it may do so whenever nothing could tell. A shader whose only outputs are its colours can be skipped for a rejected fragment, since those outputs would be thrown away. A shader with side effects, such as atomics or storage writes, cannot be skipped unseen: its writes would be missing. For such a shader the depth test comes after the shader, which is called late-Z. Every fragment the lit pass rasterises then runs the shader, atomics included, and only afterwards does the depth-equal test reject the hidden ones.

The Vulkan specification states the rule. It lists the depth test after fragment shading and moves it before only for a shader that declares the EarlyFragmentTests execution mode, and it lets an implementation skip a shader for a fragment that a later operation discards only where "the shader does not write to any storage resources". The counts were taken on the M4 Pro, through Metal, and show the same there: hidden fragments ran the counting shader. Apple GPUs render in tiles and normally work out which fragment is frontmost at each pixel before shading, so that only visible fragments shade, which is called hidden-surface removal. This guide found no Apple document that says what becomes of it for a shader with side effects; what the measurement shows is that it did not keep the hidden fragments' atomics from running. Town makes the overcount large, because its lit pass draws many hidden surfaces behind each visible one: everything both culling phases keep, and with culling off, everything.

The fix WGSL cannot reach on Metal

Standard WGSL has no way to force the test early, but naga, the shader translator wgpu uses, adds one: its @early_depth_test(force) attribute, the counterpart of SPIR-V's EarlyFragmentTests and GLSL's layout(early_fragment_tests). It needs Features::SHADER_EARLY_DEPTH_TEST, which wgpu 30 offers on Vulkan and GL only, and naga's Metal writer emits no [[early_fragment_tests]], Metal's own form of it. So on the M4 Pro, where the Budgets are judged, Metal has the fix, but a WGSL shader in wgpu 30 cannot ask for it.

Counting after the frame instead

So part 1 counts in a compute pass after the frame's others, the light count pass, behind a Render switch, count_point_lights, which praxis capture turns on and the shipping default leaves off. The lit pass normally discards its depth at the end (StoreOp::Discard); with the switch on, it stores it. One workgroup counts each 8 × 8 tile of pixels. Each invocation reads its pixel's depth at sample 0, and where the frame drew a surface, it rebuilds the surface's point, finds its Froxel from the sample's place in the pixel grid, the target's size and the view depth (froxel_at), counts the lights the Froxel lists, and, of those, the lights whose range contains the point. The workgroup sums its pixels' counts in workgroup memory, and one invocation adds each sum to the frame's light counts: one atomic add per count per workgroup. The result is exact on every backend, and the lit pass that ships is untouched.

Rebuilding the surface point

Depth in Praxis is reversed, with no far plane (ADR 0015): the projection gives clip z = near and clip w = the view depth, so the depth stored is near / view depth, and 0 where nothing was drawn. The pass inverts it, view depth = near / depth. The sample's position in normalised device coordinates, times the view depth, gives clip x and y. The camera-relative point p then satisfies three linear equations, one for each row of view_proj that depends on it; positions reach the GPU relative to the camera, so no row holds a translation:

clip.x = x_row · p clip.y = y_row · p clip.w = w_row · p p = ( clip.x (y_row × w_row) + clip.y (w_row × x_row) + clip.w (x_row × y_row) ) / ( x_row · (y_row × w_row) )

That is Cramer's rule for three equations, written with cross products: the denominator is the determinant of the three rows, and each cross product is a column of their inverse times it. WGSL has no matrix inverse and the camera uniform carries none, so the shader solves the three equations directly. It rebuilds the point where sample 0 lies in the pixel, from WebGPU's standard 4x pattern, since that is the sample whose depth it read.

Listed and reaching

A Froxel lists every point light whose range reaches anywhere into its bounds, so a listed light can still miss a given point inside it. glTF's KHR_lights_punctual requires an engine to ignore a light beyond its range, so a light out of range of the point adds nothing there, though the lit pass still loops over it; the pass that fills the shadow mask skips it without tracing a ray. The light count pass counts both.

An oracle test, the_light_count_pass_counts_the_point_lights_each_lit_pixel_lists_and_is_reached_by, renders a white quad over the left half of a 48 × 48 view. The pass counts 1,152 lit pixels. With 3 lights in front reaching past the quad, it counts 3,456 listed and 3,456 reaching. With 1 light just behind the quad, inside its Froxels' slice of depth but out of range of the quad, the light is listed, 70 times in one run, and reaches none. Each wrong version was shown to fail: counting the background too (depth ≥ 0) gives 2,304 lit pixels, and ignoring range counts the 70 as reaching.

Round the town, each lit pixel's list holds 6.59 lights on average at frame 1,059 and 6.29 at 1,859, and only 0.77 and 0.76 of them reach it. A Froxel is a fixed rectangle of the screen and a slice of depth, and the slices lengthen with each doubling of depth, so from the circle a far Froxel holds a large piece of the town and lists many of its lamps, most of which reach none of its pixels. The lit pass loops over every one of them, and so does the pass that fills the shadow mask, though it traces a ray only for the lights in range that the surface faces. The lights the mask had no bit for, which frame stats count, were none at frames 59 and 500. In the street the lists are short, 1.09 lights listed and 0.64 reaching, and on the rise they lengthen while fewer of their lights reach: 3.04 listed, 0.37 reaching.

Chapter 7 · ADR 0019's 4x MSAA

MSAA at the wires

A wire 1.5 cm in radius is thinner than a pixel from a few tens of metres away. With one sample per pixel, a pixel shows the wire only where the wire crosses that pixel's one sample, so a far wire breaks into dashes, and one far enough away disappears. With 4x MSAA each pixel tests coverage at four places and blends what they find, so a thin wire reaches more pixels, each partly covered, and stays continuous as long as it is thicker than the gaps between those places: for a level wire, the quarter of a pixel between one row of samples and the next. M3's exit asks for a one-sample render to fail the thin-geometry Golden, and town's wires make it fail.

PR #273 checked this before its rebase onto #277, on main 0743418, with a scratch patch that renders with one sample: SAMPLE_COUNT 1, the lit pass writing the HDR target directly instead of resolving into it, and depth read with one sample. Town's Golden then fails at 14,124 of its 230,400 pixels, by up to 231 8-bit steps in a channel. The difference runs along every edge in the frame, not only the wires, since every silhouette changes when its pixels lose their partial coverage.

At the wires the change is larger than a softer edge. At 4x the wires run as faint lines, each pixel showing the share of its samples a wire covers, and break only in places: a pixel or two here and there, about 20 pixels at mid-span on the upper wire across the street, which is about 48 m away and about 0.2 of a pixel thick, and runs level there between two rows of samples, and gaps of up to 13 pixels in the next pair up the avenue, about 98 m away and about half as thick, near the foot of the crop below. With one sample they break into dashes, and of the pair across the street only short dashes are left, one of them alone against the sky.

This is the kind of image ADR 0019 chose MSAA for. A post-process pass such as SMAA 1x "cannot reconstruct sub-pixel geometry": it works from the finished image, in which a missed wire is absent. The main pass reads no screen-space history either, so no earlier frame can fill the gaps. 4x MSAA has to catch the wire in every frame, and town's Golden now fails where it does not.

Chapter 8 · the Baseline on the shadow mask, for #207

What it costs

Part 3's M4 Pro Baseline, re-recorded after the rebase onto #277, is a full run of 2,048 frames at 1440p, its medians taken over the 1,800 frames the bench measures. The median frame draws enough for M3's first exit criterion, but takes 17.6 ms on the GPU, 1.5 times the 12 ms Budget, and 16.5 ms on the CPU, 2.7 times the 6 ms; its 617 MB of GPU memory are well inside 8 GB. Before #277, with the shadow rays traced inside the lit pass, the same frame took 92.7 ms on the GPU, 86.9 of them in the lit pass. The birds add about 5 ms, most of it the dynamic TLAS's build as the frame times it, which #207 later found mostly overlaps the previous frame's passes: with the build timed from the end of that frame's last pass, the GPU frame is about 13.6 ms, still past the 12 ms Budget (see the table's note). #207 judges these figures, and M3's exit re-decides ADR 0013's Budgets against them. On main, #290 has since re-recorded this Baseline with the same counts, reading 18.1 ms on the GPU and 20.2 on the CPU.

The shadow mask

Until #277, the lit pass traced each pixel's shadow rays inline, and nearly all of town's GPU frame was the lit pass: 86.9 of 92.7 ms. #277 moved the rays into a compute pass of their own. Once both culling phases' Instances are in the depth target, phase two's drawn there by a depth pass of their own, it takes each pixel's nearest sample, rebuilds that surface's position from its depth and its face normal from the neighbouring pixels' depths, and goes through the pixel's lights in the order the lit pass sums them. A point light out of range is skipped, a light the surface faces away from is hidden with no ray, and any other gets one ray: against the static TLAS and, only where that finds nothing, the dynamic TLAS. A bit for each hidden light, up to 59 a pixel, goes into the shadow mask, which the lit pass reads; the lit pass has no ray queries left. ADR 0019, amended for it, records why: traced inline, the rays cost the M4 Pro's GPU frame 2.4 to 13.4 times what they cost from the mask, on every Reference scene but texture-wall, and a ray query anywhere in the lit pass's shader cost it 0.9 to 3.9 ms even where it never ran. In town the mask now takes 4.66 ms and the lit pass 8.69.

The mask changed town's Goldens too: part 2's at 7,548 pixels on Metal and 6,433 on software Vulkan beyond the Goldens' tolerance of one 8-bit step, by up to 232 in a channel, along geometry and shadow edges, since at an edge pixel every surface's fragment now reads the bits traced from the pixel's nearest sample (ADR 0019). ADR 0019's gate is still timed from the first culling dispatch to the end of the lit pass with shadow rays off, which a Baseline traced with rays on does not give: #207 measured it on 2026-10-26 at 9.61 ms median (p95 10.15), a breach, so tickets #296 and #297 will prototype options B and D on town, and M3 cannot exit until they are done and the project's owner decides.

What the birds cost

To see what the flocks cost, town was benched four times back to back on the shadow mask, alternating with and without the birds, while the machine's CPU was heavily loaded, so the runs' CPU times are left out. The birds add about 5 ms to the GPU frame, though few triangles: without them the median frame still draws 131,470 Instances and 5,402,310 triangles, against 137,730 and 5,552,214 with them.

About 1.0 ms of the difference is the frame's own passes running longer; the rest is the dynamic TLAS's build, 4.1 ms as the frame times it, most of which, as the table's note says, overlaps the previous frame's passes; a frame with no Moving Instances does not run it. The shadow mask pass's share comes from its rays: every ray the static TLAS lets through is also traced against the dynamic TLAS, whose 10,000 entries the flocks spread over the whole town, while with no Moving Instances that second query is skipped. And nearly all of a frame's heap allocations are the birds': 10,478 with them, 341 without.

The CPU

The median CPU frame takes 16.5 ms, p95 17.4, against the 6 ms Budget, recorded with a load average of about 3 on the machine. Recording and submitting the dynamic TLAS's build take 1.00 ms of it, p95 1.11, above the 0.64 to 0.92 ms that ADR 0020 gives for its recording plus submit. An earlier, indicative measurement on part 2's first push put 12.4 ms of a 14 ms CPU frame in the frame's submit, and #204's hand-off to #207 explains why: at submit, wgpu-core walks the dependencies of each TLAS for every pass that sets a bind group holding it, one per Instance, and Metal adds each to a new residency set. The hand-off counted four such passes. Since #277, six set the group that holds both TLAS: both culling phases, the depth pre-pass, phase two's depth pass, the shadow mask pass and the lit pass.

Each frame uploads 2,049,344 bytes, nearly all of it for the birds: 10,000 Instances of 128 bytes and 10,000 Metal TLAS entries of 72. And each frame makes 10,478 heap allocations, nearly one per dynamic TLAS entry: wgpu-core converts every entry with wgpu-hal's tlas_instance_to_bytes, which returns a new Vec each time.

The culling phases

The two culling phases took 1.51 and 0.90 ms, each dispatching a workgroup per Instance slot in use, 254,256 of them, with an invocation per triangle of a Meshlet. The culling shader carries a comment from #194 for exactly this case: "a workgroup per Instance idles most invocations on small Meshes; split it into a pass per Instance and one per Meshlet when a profile of a dense scene names it". Town is that dense scene, and whether its profile names it is #207's call.

Reference

Glossary

Reference scene
A Scene paired with a fixed camera path and injected time, so that every run produces the same frames.
Golden
A committed image that a Reference scene must reproduce within tolerance.
Baseline
The recorded measurements of a Reference scene on one adapter: the reference machine, where Budgets are judged against it, or CI's lavapipe, where only its counters are judged.
Budget
A numeric limit on one metric of a Reference scene (CPU ms, GPU ms, GPU memory, Input latency) that a change must not regress.
Instance
One Renderable's data on the GPU: its World transform, as an Anchor plus an f32 offset, its Mesh, and the Material ID that overrides its Mesh's Materials, if any, in a slot of the Instance buffer that the Renderable keeps while it is one.
Moving Instance
An Instance whose entity the fixed schedule moves, so the entity carries its previous World transform.
Renderable
An entity that has a Transform and a Mesh Handle, and optionally a Material Handle that overrides the Materials of its Mesh's Primitives.
Mesh
An ordered list of Primitives that are rendered together.
Primitive
One contiguous range of a Mesh's vertices and indices.
Meshlet
A small group of one Primitive's triangles, bounded in vertex and triangle count, that the GPU draws as a unit and can cull as one.
Material
The appearance of a Primitive: PBR factors plus one Layer per Channel.
Placement
One Mesh Asset positioned inside a Chunk.
Chunk
A fixed-size vertical column of the Game world, addressed by integer coordinates, that loads and unloads as a unit.
Blob
One cooked Asset's bytes, named by their content hash.
Cooked cache
The store of Blobs plus the index from Asset ID to Blob.
Asset ID
The stable name of an Asset, derived from its source file's GUID, or a procedural Reference scene's name.
Tick
One fixed step of the simulation, 1/64 s; rendered frames draw a blend of the two newest Ticks.
World transform
Where an entity sits in a Game world: its Transform composed with every ancestor's, in f64.
Froxel
A cell of the view frustum, bounded in screen space and depth, that holds the list of lights reaching it.
Frame stats
What one frame cost, measured by the engine itself.
Render switches
The variants a Renderer is created with and keeps.
Capability floor
The GPU features and limits the engine requires to start. An adapter below it is refused with every gap named; nothing in it has a fallback.
SplitMix64
Vigna's fixed-increment version of Steele, Lea and Flood's SplitMix generator: a state that grows by a fixed odd constant each step, returned scrambled; town hashes with one step.
Random value
In town.rs, a number from 0 up to 1 that random hashes from a column and row, a choice and an item.
Choice
What a random value decides, from a park to a bird; the value hashes it by its position in the enum.
Hermite curve
The cubic curve fixed by a position and a velocity at each of its two ends.
TLAS
Top-level acceleration structure: the GPU's search structure over Instances, each placing a BLAS with its transform.
BLAS
Bottom-level acceleration structure: the GPU's search structure over one Mesh's triangles.
Static TLAS
The top-level acceleration structure over every static Instance whose Mesh rays trace, each placing its Mesh's BLAS and tagged with its slot.
Dynamic TLAS
The top-level acceleration structure over the Moving Instances, at most 10,000 of them, rebuilt every frame; town's birds fill it.
Hi-Z pyramid
Ever smaller copies of the frame's depth, each texel the farthest beneath it, that culling tests bounds against.
Packed index
One u32 of the frame's draw: a Meshlet record in its high bits, a vertex within that Meshlet in its low bits.
Lit pass
The pass that shades each pixel at depth equal after the depth pre-pass, reading each light's shadow from the shadow mask.
Shadow mask
A bit per light at each pixel, set where that light is hidden from the surface the pixel's nearest sample shows, by the surface itself where it faces away or by what its shadow ray finds, in the order the lit pass sums the pixel's lights, up to 59 of them.
Late-Z
Testing depth after the fragment shader has run, as a GPU must for a shader with side effects that does not ask for the test early.
Hidden-surface removal
On a tile-based deferred GPU, working out the frontmost fragment at each pixel before shading, so only it shades.
Light count pass
Part 1's compute pass that counts, at each lit pixel, the point lights listed and those reaching it.
Listed, reaching
Lights a pixel's Froxel lists, and those of them whose range contains the pixel's surface point.
Cramer's rule
Solving linear equations as ratios of determinants; for three, with cross products and one dot product.
